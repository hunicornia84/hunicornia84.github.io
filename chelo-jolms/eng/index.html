A note on the grammar of this translation

Spanish gives almost every noun and adjective a gender, so a Spanish sentence obliges whoever speaks it to gender everything and everyone in it. Chelo Jolms refuses. Throughout the original, Chelo bends those endings — asesine, chique, blanqui, todes — replacing the masculine -o and the feminine -a with forms Spanish does not officially have. Two of them, the -e and the -x, circulate in Spanish today. The rest are Chelo's own.

English has no such endings to bend. Its nouns and adjectives carry no gender at all, so a faithful English sentence would quietly erase the entire argument. This translation therefore invents what English lacks: a suffix, -i, attached to the word that names or describes anyone who refuses the binary. Killeri. Kidi. Whiti. All of usi. Nothing else in the grammar has been touched — Chelo is he in the narration here exactly as he is él there.

Cuir is left as Chelo writes it. It is queer, respelled as the word sounds in Spanish: a Latin American mark of distance from queer theory as produced in the United States, and a word this novel argues with at length.

Bending English to this end is not new. Ursula K. Le Guin gave the ambisexual Gethenians of The Left Hand of Darkness masculine pronouns in 1969, and spent decades arguing with that decision — in the essay “Is Gender Necessary?” and its later revision, and by reprinting a Gethen story with the pronouns turned feminine. Ann Leckie made she the default for every character in Ancillary Justice, and took the Hugo, the Nebula and the Arthur C. Clarke for it. Chelo, who at one point identifies a perfume named after Le Guin's The Dispossessed, is in reasonable company.
