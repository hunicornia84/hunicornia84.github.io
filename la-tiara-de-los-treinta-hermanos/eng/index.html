The Tiara of the Thirty Brothers

A note on this edition. This story was written in Spanish. The glosses in brackets are the translator’s, not the author’s. The narrator is Colombian, and the words that mark her as such (mamá, papá, the double given names, the diminutives, the names her family gave each other) have been kept in Spanish rather than translated away, so that she goes on speaking from where she speaks.

After a couple of hours in the car, between valleys and mountains lashed by that warm autumn levante, the golden colours of Mediterranean gardens about it, the sign announced the exit some five hundred metres ahead. I drove along a quiet A-road for another half hour and, after skirting the town from one side to the other, I found a small marker directing me down a narrow gravel track hedged with artichoke and onion plots; the smell of green thinned out the thick feel of city air.

The plain opened out wide and a small hamlet of few houses, lived in by those farm labourers, stood as the last human outpost before the view lost itself among the ranges of pine and rosemary.

It didn’t look hard to find the place I was after. An old man was watching me, recognising an outsider.

― Forgive me, sir. The main street, if you would be so kind? -he gave me a guarded look.

― We’ve none of that here, all the streets are the same. The chapel, though, and the square and the bar are in the middle of the village. Turn that corner there and a few metres on you’ll come upon them.

The man touched his beret and walked on, making a toothpick dance between his lips. I didn’t come across another soul until I reached the square, where there were two benches so full it was comical how tightly packed they were, the old men on one side, the old women on the other; they talked like parakeets, shielding themselves from the sun with fans, while the men sat in silence with their eyes lost. When I passed between them I felt the weight of their looks, which did not let up until I had gone through the door of the bar.

The counter was empty; at one table two guiris (foreign tourists) were chatting beside a pile of empty pint glasses. Four old men were playing dominoes. In one corner a woman of about seventy was attending to the programme the television was putting out. I sat on a stool and waited; a sweating man in overalls came into the bar and sat down beside me.

― Linda, Linda! Get me a beer.

― Coming! -answered a young voice from the kitchen.

A girl of about twenty, dark-haired, cinnamon-skinned and grey-eyed, with one of those smiles that brighten the day, came through the curtain carrying several trays, which she put away in the display cabinets. She took a glass, filled it and handed it to him. She took two steps back towards the kitchen and stopped, turning slowly, and looked at me.

― Well now! I didn’t even notice you were sitting there, do forgive me… never a moment’s peace…

― That’s quite all right -she smiled at me and I could do nothing but give her a smile from ear to ear.

― What would you like?

― Two fingers of honey rum with ice.

― Goodness, you’re the first Spaniard ever to ask me for that.

― I find it easier to accept that the drink is killing me sweetly.

The girl was about to pour the liquid when a whistle interrupted her; she left the bottle and went over to the woman, who whispered something in her ear without taking her eyes off the television. The girl came back behind the counter, rummaged in a drawer, took out what looked like half the shell of a seed, rinsed it and, in front of me, filled it with the rum.

― …A jícara (a gourd cup)? I had heard of them, but I had never drunk from one.

― Rum tastes better in here than in a glass… in fact any American drink tastes better served in a shell.

― And the ice…?

― Try it -she was smiling, and although I dislike warm spirits, I drank.

― Mmm… different… For today I’ll allow it, but I’m afraid that at my age ice is a good habit and hard to root out. Forgive me for keeping you a little longer.

― No, it’s quite all right.

― I’m looking for the woman who runs this bar. If I remember rightly her name is Beatriz Milagros. I sense it can’t be you, knowing your name is Linda and given your barefaced youth.

― Quite so, my name is Rosalinda Camila, and that lady over there is the woman you’re looking for. I’m the help. I’ve a feeling she already knows you came looking for her, but she’s watching her novela now, there’s barely fifteen minutes left of it. You can read the paper if you like, to make the wait more agreeable. Here you are, one from the left and one from the right.

― Thank you, but I’m afraid that apart from the sudoku there’s little of interest in either. Have you read them?

― I hardly read at all, I spend many hours here in the bar. But my teacher from the secondary school, who’s a customer, comes in every day to inform me with “Le Monde diplomatique” under his arm. He feels guilty because I left my studies just short of finishing them, and he keeps me up to date. I’m grateful to him, though I don’t pay him much attention. For being a barmaid what I know is more than enough, so I leave the thinking and the acting to the intellectuals. I get myself worked up hearing about so many thieves and liars. Leave me in peace, I’m happier being a fool. Look at my teacher, bald from so much thinking -she said, while she washed the glasses.

― That’s a respectable outlook, I practise it often myself, which is why I still have hair at nearly fifty.

― You’re looking very well for someone so old.

― Oof! I suppose I ought to thank you.

― Ha, ha, ha, it’s a joke, you look well. My papá is younger than you and he already looks like a dried-out fig.

― I hope you don’t say that in front of him.

― There’s no need, my mother says it to him every day.

Something was whistling in the kitchen and the girl went off inside. The novela was still running, so I took out my book and opened it at the marked page. The girl reappeared before I had got to the end of the first line.

― And what is it you’re reading? -she asked, draped languidly over the counter, turning the pages of the book.

― Rayuela (Hopscotch).

― A very short title for such a long book.

― Quite so, perhaps it ought to be called “Instructions for jumping from one square to the next without treading on the line or losing your reason altogether”. Very lengthy, but entertaining. Interesting.

― I hope it is. When you’ve finished it, do come back and tell me about it.

― I’ll hold you to that.

― Look, the serial’s over. Wait a minute and take her this mint and liquorice, she always has one at this hour -the girl made up the infusion and handed it to me; I took it and went over to the table.

― Good morning, may I?

― By all means, take a seat.

― Thank you.

― What can I do for you?

― I’m Doménico. I don’t know whether Mercedes Consuelo said anything to you about me; indeed I apologise for turning up unannounced, but I have always believed that anything prepared loses its freshness.

Your stories reached me through my sister, who is a friend of the daughter of a cousin of the aforementioned. And although after much insisting and digging I got hold of the bar’s telephone number, I preferred to present myself and hear your story in your own voice -she looked at me slackly, drinking her infusion.

― And what do you know about me?

― Very little, to be honest. I’m a librarian, I love gathering information about everything that trespasses the barriers of this reality we live in. I write these accounts down and set them out for anyone who wants to read them, but I’m not a writer, I confine myself to setting down every legend and fantasy worth knowing. And from what I gather, the story of your life unfolds wrapped in a halo of mystery, magic, music and love.

― And what makes you think I’m going to narrate my life to a complete stranger?

― Erm… I’ve no answer to that. It’s true that in the end it depends on you. I’ve been forward in taking the liberty. All I can do is confirm my good faith and my enormous interest, and leave your decision to chance. But I warn you: if you accept, I shall put your story on the same shelf as Julio Cortázar -she finishes drinking and sets the cup on the table, sighs deeply.

― I know some work of that gentleman’s, but before you put my story beside his books, ask him whether he wants my life sitting next to his -I looked at her, delighted. She looked calmly at the clock-. It’s twelve o’clock; I imagine two hours will be more than enough to satisfy your longings, and leave me free for lunchtime.

― Of course!! -I exclaimed, thrilled-. I wouldn’t want to be a nuisance or steal a second more than I must. For me it’s a gift that you accept.

The woman got up and went over to the counter, exchanged a few words with the girl that I couldn’t make out, and headed for the stairs beside the door to the toilets. On the third step a screen of woven palm blocked the way; she moved it and with a nod of her head invited me to follow her. The staircase was very steep and struck me as long for the height the place seemed to have. The corridor turned left and gave onto a double door; she took a key from her pocket, opened it and went in, leaving me outside. A moment later she called me; I looked in. It was full of crates of soft drinks, beer and tinned goods. She was raising the last of the blinds, letting in a lovely light that thickened over the dust hanging in the air. There were two armchairs with their footstools, one facing the other.

― Close the door and sit down -I waited for her to take her seat-. Have you brought paper and pencil?

― Yes, I have a blank notebook, but if you’ll allow me I’d rather record your account, so that I can transcribe it calmly without losing a single detail, keeping the warm accent of each of your words, building every sentence the same way you build it, distilling the perfume of its protagonist. I took the recorder out of my briefcase and set it on a stack of small bottles beside her. I sat down and settled in to enjoy the telling.

― Are you recording already?

― Mm-hm…

― Good…

Allow me to go back to the beginning of this story, back in the twenties of the last century. My grandfather, José Fernando by name, was a handsome man, gallant and wealthy; among his possessions he had a fine and good wife who gave him five children, three of them female. He also had several plots of land rented out for cultivation and a haulage company that shipped the tastiest fruit and vegetables all over the continent, which allowed him a life full of whims.

But my lord grandfather had the bad habit, without my grandmother knowing, of lingering after work in a room where people of influence did their dealing outside the law. And there, in that place where the flashing red light of the sign read “Poker Gang Club”, my grandfather lost everything he had, his money, his dignity and possibly his underpants too, staking his uncontrollable ambition on a single card. He went home not knowing what would become of his life, thinking what to tell his wife and his five little ones.

Walking him through the night of the great city of Manizales was a mangy mongrel, the special gift the mafias give to those who owe them the very air they breathe. My grandmother knew nothing of this detail when she saw her husband come into the house white-faced in the company of the mutt. Her guilelessness was this gentleman’s best argument; he sat her down in an armchair and, with the help of a good slug of aguardiente, had her believe the following fabrication.

― Listen to me, Ángela Gabriela, today I went back to my office as I do every day, to run my company and count up the fruit of my labours. Night had come and the employees had gone, leaving me alone in the office, and when I came out I found this dog sitting in the doorway. It had a little letter tied round its neck, addressed to me, “Att. J.F.”, and inside all it said was “Out”, and as sender, “COC (Colombian Organised Crime)”. When I went out into the street with the dog behind me, I ran looking for an officer and told him what had happened, showing him the letter, and he put his hands to his head and warned me that the mafia that rules this city has its eye on my successful career. We have no choice but to leave the city and abandon everything we own if we don’t want to end up bones in the cemetery inside five days. He told me we must be quick and leave without delay, since the police have next to no power over organised crime and will do nothing even if we report it.

My grandmother showed herself to be more guileless than one might expect, or perhaps, knowing everything, she preferred not to argue over what was already lost. All that night she packed cases and boxed up everything they could carry. The next morning, very early, the seven of them were already waiting at the bus stop that was to take them to the most out-of-the-way place in Colombia, there to begin a new life.

They left with what they stood up in and a handful of dollars my grandmother kept hidden from her husband; that was all the money they had. Five hours later they were stepping down from the coach in a small village whose name no map knew. It was a place where you went back at least a few decades in time. Fortunately money was worth more here than in the city, and after a couple of days living in the priest’s house, my grandfather struck a good deal with a widowed farmer, old and childless, who owned a small plot with a vegetable garden and a house with red adobe walls and a cement floor.

It had eight rooms, with bunk beds three mattresses high in three of them, several single cots and one double bed. A kitchen with two washing troughs, a well to draw water from, a larder, a table for ten and a privy in the back yard made of corrugated cement sheets. Along with a couple of suckling kids the old man could no longer look after. The deeds were signed under the priest’s supervision, and from that day that house became our family’s shelter.

The first night the two of them slept in their new home, my grandmother gave her husband an ultimatum. If he wanted to go on seeing his children, his wife, and having a house to live in, he would have to forget drink and gambling entirely, work hard to feed the mouths of his family, and let her be the one to administer every coin that came into the house. My grandfather accepted without a murmur, and that man of doubtful morals stayed behind in Manizales, giving way to a new one. Every day he worked the garden, looked after the goats and a couple of laying hens. He also worked in a small fruit store where he helped gather avocados, pineapples and bananas, loaded crates and vans and handled the accounts. He left at first light and came back late at night, filthy and smelling of sweat. Hard work, without rest, rewarded with aches enough to cover the most basic needs.

My grandmother, for her part, who had been a teacher in the city, took to looking after children, as well as teaching them to read and write. Although they took a while to get used to their new way of life, their neighbours accepted them warmly straight away, valuing their qualities.

When my grandmother started working she had nobody to leave her children with, so she took the lot of them wherever she went. She looked after other people’s children, and the old, she cooked lunches and dinners and cleaned until the last hour of daylight was used up. Such was the ruin they had been left in, and so many the mouths to feed, that my grandparents could only buy a garment when one of the little ones had a growth spurt, and the clothes passed from one sibling to the next, ever more patched and faded. The girls dressed like little boys, but to tell them apart she tied them a pretty ribbon and hung a pretty flower brooch on their chests. With shoes, though, it didn’t go the same way: once the ones they had brought from the city fell apart, what with the good climate and the ground soft as sand, the children were left with no shoes to put on and went out into the street with bare feet, which though strange to their parents was the height of fashion among the children of that poor-caste neighbourhood.

My grandmother, when she finally wore her own shoes out, set aside her heels in exchange for a pair of esparto and palm sandals, which displeased her enormously, though at least they kept her skin off the earth.

That new life of the poor prince stretched on so long that little by little the children grew up, and as they got older they were sent out to work to bring money into the house. By then my mother was about fourteen and worked alongside my grandmother, sharing the chores with her and doing whatever my grandmother, on account of her age, could no longer manage. My mamá always had a special relationship with her mother. She had inherited her kindness, her generosity, her beauty and that everlasting smile that never turned away anyone who needed help. She used to say that my grandmother had a sixth sense and always smelt out the truth in whatever seemed to be hiding it, perhaps only a woman’s intuition, perhaps a touch of the witch that my mamá inherited too, but let’s leave that for later.

My grandmother, among others, looked after a very old lady who lived alone, since her children lived in the capitals, each keeping up with the others by letter. My mamá used to read those lines out, and doña Renata Esperanza was delighted to hear the sweet voice of the little girl who read so well. But on one of those days the news that came was of a great misfortune, for Sergio Alejandro, the youngest of the sons, was announcing the death of his wife after a short and incurable illness. The old lady wept for her daughter-in-law’s death and for her son’s life to come, a man in love who had lost his wife and was left in charge of no end of children at forty-nine. Such was the sorrow Renata Esperanza felt that my grandmother, unable to leave her alone, asked my mamá to stay and look after the lady for a few days until her son came to visit her.

For six days my mamá kept the little old lady company morning and night. In the many hours they shared, my mamá came to know of Sergio Alejandro, a man in love with music who played more than ten instruments, an artist in his youth who, forced by his father, ended up studying to become a civil engineer and a man of good capital. Shortly after qualifying he met his wife, and with her he had a great number of children; four of them had died before reaching ten years of age, six were already living on their own, and the other four were eight, seven, five and two, still in need of a woman’s care. My mamá breathed more easily; the old lady’s despondency had led her young imagination to picture that man looking after fourteen children still in nappies.

A few days later the man appeared at the house with his four little ones; the door sounded, Renata Esperanza had dozed off, so it was my mother who opened it, and on the other side a gentleman stood expectant with his eyes fixed on the girl’s while the children scampered inside the house.

― Good morning.

― Good morning, I am Sergio Alejandro, Renata Esperanza’s son, and you are?

― My name is María Eladia, but I prefer Mariela. I look after your mamá a few hours a day, but since she heard of your wife’s death I have stayed here waiting for you to come.

My deepest condolences -he said, kissing her cheek. The man came into the house with his bearing hampered by the girl’s great charm.

― Thank you very much. A pleasure to meet you…

― Sergio Alejandro!! -cried the old lady, running down the corridor in floods of tears to throw herself upon him.

The old woman monopolised her son and her grandchildren and talked and talked until well into the night, so my mamá left without anyone missing her.

The next morning my grandmother was making breakfast when a well-dressed and very handsome man, carrying two bunches of flowers, tapped on the kitchen window.

― Good morning, forgive the intrusion, I’m Renata Esperanza’s son. I know you’re leaving for work, but I wanted to thank you in person for looking after my mamá.

― Ah! Of course, do come in and have breakfast with us, please -she opened the back door and brought him inside.

The man offered her the flowers, which she put in a vase. In a few minutes the whole family appeared and Sergio Alejandro was introduced. With a smiling look he offered the other bunch to Mariela, who left them in her lap while they all had breakfast and listened to the engineer’s adventures in the city. The visit was brief, and each of them went off to work.

During those days doña Renata Esperanza did without her care, for she was so happy with her son that she seemed to have gone back to her youth. He had meant to stay only a few days, and on each of them he came out onto the porch to greet my mamá on her way back.

― Good evening, señorita Mariela, sleep well.

― Good evening, don Sergio Alejandro, and you.

The week went by, and on the day the cases should have been packed for his return he lingered to see the girl come back from work once more. I don’t know what got into my papá, at his age, to fall in love with a slip of a girl, even knowing that my mother was a lovely and adorable young woman. What happened was that on one of those afternoons my papá waited for daughter and mother to come back, stopped them both there and offered my grandmother a pair of red patent shoes and my mamá a pair of pretty clogs. Ángela Gabriela was smitten and poured out her thanks, but Mariela accepted them with a certain wariness.

― I should like you to accept these presents, some lovely shoes for two even lovelier ladies. To show my gratitude for looking after my mamá.

― Oh, thank you so much! But you shouldn’t have.

― I saw that you go about, Ángela Gabriela, in those palm sandals, and little Mariela barefoot with those tiny little feet, and I said to myself, “I must give them a present they like and that will serve to make them remember me every day”, and so it was.

― Thank you very much, you’ve chosen perfectly -she said, putting on the red shoes, which she even wore to clean the house.

My mamá, however, who had walked barefoot for so many years, put them on out of politeness as she passed the old lady’s house, where the engineer was waiting for her, and took them off immediately afterwards, waiting for him to leave so she could put them away at the far end of the wardrobe. But the days went by, and my grandmother went over to ask what it was that kept him at his mother’s side, and great was her surprise when she heard the answer, for she had taken it for granted that the man was so stricken and sad that he wished to give himself more time; but what Sergio Alejandro said to her was this.

― Look, doña Ángela Gabriela, if I haven’t left it isn’t because of my sadness, for that is something I shall carry with me wherever I go, nor because I wish to stay with my mother, however much joy it gives me to see her. I know it will sound like madness coming from a widower of nearly half a century, with children in my charge and children much older than she is, but from the moment I saw her I fell in love…

― You are very flattering, but I am a married woman and faithful to my husband, so do not think for one moment…

― No, no, I’m afraid I explained myself badly -and she listened, more surprised still-. If I were in love with you it would be sensible; the madness is that I have fallen in love with your little Mariela.

― Now I understand -she said drily.

― Say nothing to her, for without your husband’s consent and your own I did not dare go further. But since you came over, I wanted to seize the occasion.

― Let me think -was the only answer my grandmother gave, leaving the house.

In those years it was very common to see girls married off to grown men, but thirty-five years’ difference was perhaps too many to answer without consulting. That night Ángela Gabriela spoke with José Fernando and they came to the conclusion that although he was a good man by his character and his well-off work, he was already elderly, perhaps unable to give her more children and with several of his own in his charge, a man who, rather than look after their daughter, would have to be looked after by her.

Although they seemed clear about the refusal, they had at least to tell their daughter what the engineer’s intentions were. At breakfast the next morning my mamá was informed, but so young and inexperienced was she that she did not know what to say. She kept her silence for three days.

― Mother, father, I have made a decision. I am going to give him the chance to go out walking with me on Sundays, and if I like him perhaps we’ll marry. He’s a handsome gentleman and he has money; if he has offspring and he’s old, it makes no odds, the only thing I know how to do is look after people and it won’t feel strange to me.

My grandparents gave the girl their consent, and that is how they began to see each other as friends. She was the one who told him the decision, and the light of eighteen shone again in his eyes, which were full of joy. Now that he had decided to stay, he would travel to see to his work and be back for Sunday.

One sunny Sunday, at the fountain in the square where they had first met. He waited standing, in his suit, with a bunch of white roses; she wore a green dress, a pearl pendant and her hair loose in the wind.

― You look ever so pretty, Mariela, but I couldn’t help noticing you’re barefoot.

― Look, don Sergio Alejandro, I am greatly grateful to you for the gesture, but however hard I try, they make me feel uncomfortable. I was waiting for you to leave, but seeing as you’re staying, you should know I shall keep them well away from my feet.

They went on seeing each other every Sunday for two years without missing a single appointment. On one of those days he asked her to come and live with him and my mamá accepted. That same afternoon he presented himself at my grandparents’ house; he was looking very handsome, dashing, with his green eyes glinting, his cinnamon skin and the grey covering his temples, so handsome that her papás could not refuse their dear daughter.

After the consent, my parents began living together in a little house they had bought at the end of the street. Mariela began to dress like a woman and little by little got used to wearing shoes, at least on feast days.

It may be that in the first years my mamá didn’t know what she was doing in the company of that man, looking after his four children, but as the years went by she discovered in him a good man, generous, a dreamer, who ended up making her fall in love, and that love arrived at nineteen along with her first baby. Great was the joy among them all, and great too the surprise that at his age he was still able to father children. The fact is that Mariela began looking after her home, her stepchildren, her children, other people’s children, her animals and the ones the neighbours left behind.

My house was a place everyone in the village passed through; they all knew María Eladia; who had not been cared for by her and by Ángela Gabriela, and to whom had she ever refused help when it was needed. So my house turned into a nursery where some of the old folk spent the time and where pigs, goats and hens wandered from one side to the other, while Mariela did the washing, made the food and put the home in order. I remember my mamá saying that in my house there was such a commotion and such variety that all it lacked was a couple of trained fleas to charge admission.

My papá went on working in the capital during the week, so my mamá only had half the neighbourhood in her charge on those days. Saturday and Sunday she devoted entirely to enjoying her husband. And I say husband because after their first child, Mariela and don Sergio Alejandro were married on an ordinary Sunday. The priest officiated a wedding of barely fifteen guests, children included. My mother used to say it was a lovely ceremony, simple, short and very happy.

The rest of her life did not change much; she inherited the teaching from my grandmother, drilling every child in the neighbourhood and entertaining the four old people who tried to learn if only to stave off the boredom.

The passing of time in my mother was measured by her pregnancies; with each new child she confirmed how fast the years were going. But she did not complain, she loved being surrounded, devoting her existence to others; for her, to feel alive was to be given the smile of everyone around her.

My mamá counted fourteen stepchildren; four had died, so she never knew them, six were already living on their own account, and four were in her care. To these she added seven of her own, of whom one little girl died months after being born, and nine adopted children found in the street.

Don Sergio Alejandro loved doña Mariela a great deal, but working far from home turned him gradually into a solitary man, who gave his hours to drowning himself in drink, blurring his gaze behind tobacco smoke while he played the piano in a jazz club at night. My papá’s vigour dwindled, and he came home taciturn. Until one day, newly retired, having got through all the money in his pockets, he ended up half drunk in an evangelical church, where on hearing the pastor’s word he decided to give up every vice and devote every second of his life to his wife until the last of his days.

But until that moment came, my mamá suffered beyond telling. Her husband was a gentleman who passed through her bed on Saturday nights, got her with child and disappeared until the following week. She lived that side of things sadly, but she was full of vitality, and so great was the love she needed to give that she thought nothing of putting up ten more rooms to turn her home into a refuge where anyone who needed a plate of food or a bed to rest in could find one.

So much so that, living five streets from the area where men and women worked at night, one of them turned up with company and asked my mamá whether she could rent a room for a while. Doña Mariela said yes, and after that one many more came. The rooms at the back were the ones they used, where the children heard nothing, and the money that came in was spent on the people most in need.

In my house there was never any shortage of food, nor any garment to wear; we lived humbly but well. Perhaps for that reason, because there was always food on the table and a mother brimming with affection, it happened that one of those prostitutes turned up one afternoon with her two children, two little Black boys of one and two years old, black as soot.

― Señora Mariela, I come here with my two children since I have nobody to leave them with; my mamá has fallen ill, she’s dying and I must go for a few days until she passes. If you would be so kind as to keep my little ones for a week or so, I would be most grateful, and I’ll leave you money to cover your costs over those days.

My mamá nodded, trusting; she never thought ill of anyone, but as the weeks went by, and the months, and those children went on growing up under her skirts, her guilelessness dwindled. After the Black boys, many other women came who were unable to bring their babies up, inventing a thousand excuses for my mamá to take charge of the little ones. “Señora Mariela, would you mind having them while I work”, “Oh, Mariela! Could you give them something to eat, I promise I’ll come back later”; my mother accepted, knowing full well she was being deceived, since she could not bear to see those offspring bereft of a mother’s love. And that is how Mariela came to have nine adopted children, whom she registered with my family’s surnames, all of them boys.

By the time I was born, many years later, my mamá counted fourteen children to her name. My papá was a man in his seventies devoted to his home and his family. He had been a very hard-working and lively man, though his energies were no longer those of his youth, and despite his age he still had strength enough to romp with my mother and make up for lost time. Mariela in those years was living with the first symptoms of the menopause, and her body began to change with no explanation; her belly swelled by the day and the worry took her to the doctor, who, like her, was convinced it was nothing more than another of the phenomena that come with ceasing to be fertile. And yet, having accepted the learned man’s words, at the end of the seventh month she could only be astonished when, by surprise, from between her damp legs there slipped a purplish little lizard that escaped her belly to the sound of a loud sneeze. Several days later, after my parents accepted the proposal, we married in the church and afterwards under the tribe’s ritual, where I was rebaptised and where I was taught what I know today. By him I had seven children, and while he lived he made me a very happy woman. Unfortunately he died the victim of a very powerful witchcraft that would have finished me too had I not made a pact with Pedro Botero (the Devil himself). I obtained great powers in exchange for my soul, which let me hunt down the whore who widowed me and pay her back in her own coin.

― … So then, will you go to hell?

― I suppose so, for a long spell, on account of the harm I did for money, but I’ll dodge a few years of the sentence since I also made great sacrifices out of pure altruism. The thing is to keep the balance so the scales don’t punish me eternally. In fact, I keep a bulky book, a book that holds everything I know and where I write down the new enchantments and curses I learn at the world witches’ convention held every three years in Holland. That book is part of me and part of my power lives in it; while its pages are intact, there is no curse and no illness that can get the better of me. That said, on the day of my death that volume must be burned with me when I am cremated. My body and those letters must disappear, to stop anyone using my remains to tie me to this earth.

― You leave me astonished. Did you know all this before you became a witch?

― I was born a witch, I knew the magic was burning inside me and that was what drew the indigenous man to me. Your mamá knew all about this and she did well to keep you away from it and to use her intuition to protect you all, and protection is the only thing you ought to learn -I nodded, and that was my only answer-. A witch’s duties begin at the door of her consulting room, on which there hangs a sign that says “Come in without knocking, and remember to enter backwards as a mark of respect”.

― And what happens if they come in facing forward?

― Why, they meet the gaze of the warriors who guard the entrance against evil spirits, and whoever looks a guardian in the eye suffers misfortune.

― And what are these guardians like?

― They are small figures that by the dozen take up the walls, the ceiling and the floor of the corridor leading to the waiting room.

One of the purifications that cost me most dearly was curing a lady who after an accident had been left hunchbacked, suffering great pains that kept her from working and caring for her children. Her story moved me, and although she meant to pay, I took no payment. The ritual began, and as the curve of her back straightened, mine twisted, suffering great ills; I screamed as I had never screamed, and more than five hours later the woman left completely healed. I spent two weeks battling the illness before I could get my own posture back.

In the consulting room I tell the future to whoever comes asking for a reading of the cards, of the hands, of the coffee grounds, and the truth is I usually get it right with some precision, so much so that I know well the day, the how and the place of my death. I assure you I did not enjoy seeing it.

― I’d rather leave that alone, then my death will be a surprise.

― There are lesser enchantments too, such as the obtaining of love and of hatred. You take a photograph of the one who is to feel it, and looking at the portrait you smoke a good cigar whose smoke gets inside their being and drowns them in bliss or in rage. This is much in demand among girls who are scorned by the man they love; after a couple of cigars they usually end up married to them, ha, ha, ha.

Besides curing and turning feelings around, dark sorcery is much practised; what I prepare most is salt, coffee and sugar. The salt you must throw at their feet to salt their existence, the coffee must fall on their skin so their body fills with great black blotches, and the sugar they must take so that their senses are damaged.

― The truth is that the more I learn about how you use magic, the more I feel like knowing nothing at all and giving you a kick up the backside for doing harm to people.

― With a good wad of money we’ll leave them deaf, blind and simple if need be…

I told you already, Milagros, magic is a dangerous path and I am lost already. Use it always to do good. Magic is neither black nor white, nor am I so vile; I merely serve as the executor of the wishes of whoever pays me. There are murderers, rapists, thieves whose acts make mine look small, believe me.

Let me tell you something that made me go deeper into the macabre side of witchcraft. I had seven children, six of them living. Belinda Helena, my youngest girl, the light of my eyes, was murdered. A bastard kidnapped her when she was thirteen, on her way back from school; he raped her, beat her so badly he left her unrecognisable and finally cut her throat so she couldn’t give him away. The police never found the culprit, but I did. While I was in the morgue washing her and laying her out, I tied her two big toes together with a ribbon made from a tear in the dress she was wearing at the moment of her death, and the murderer’s name was heard from her lips. It was a very hard moment that left its mark on the rest of my life. I had lost my mamá when I was a girl, and my husband, and my little one; my existence had neither comfort nor reason, and here I am being what I am, a witch with no heart.

I learned to call up the dead from purgatory, hardened souls full of hatred whose only end is to torment in life whoever they fasten on to. I looked for the murderer and I found him, I invited him to eat at my house, I got him drunk until he fell flat on his face, and while he slept I put into his circle as many dead as I could capture, and they were many. To this day that bastard is over a hundred years old, he is skin and bone, almost a corpse. He has tried to hang himself, shoot himself, poison himself, but he does not die. He has begged me, but I am not going to let him go until the day of my death, and until then he must suffer the most maddening pain every second of his existence. When I pass on, I intend to take him with me to hell.

― Goodness! I didn’t know, I’m so sorry.

― Thank you. When I cross to the other side they’ll hold my acts against me, but you can be sure I’ll be asking for explanations of my own. Well, let’s change the subject and move on to another of my clients’ adventures.

There was a girl, none too favoured in the face and well fleshed out, who came asking for some brew to take revenge on a bullfighter she was in love with. She used to say very pretty things to him every time he walked past her house.

― Oh, Tomasito! How handsome you look today, that traje de luces (the bullfighter’s suit of lights) makes you look like a star.

― How many times must I tell you I don’t like ugly, fat women? -he would answer without looking her in the face, spitting as he passed.

Every day she waited, hoping his heart would soften and that on one of those occasions he would make some kind gesture, but that moment never came, and so much rebuffing ended up wounding the girl’s heart to death, so that she stopped loving him and felt a deep hatred. So she made buñuelos with a batter mixed with a few drops of one of my potions.

― Good morning, doña Romelia Inmaculada, I’ve brought you some little buñuelos here for Tomasito. These ones, which were made with all my love, are for him alone; for you and your husband I made these here, which are just as tasty but without sugar, since I know it doesn’t agree with you.

― Thank you very much! We’ll enjoy every last one of them.

The papás ate the ones meant for them and Tomasito his. Within a few weeks he, who had been so thin, grew so fat he looked like a pig, and not one girl looked at him any more. And he learned to see with other eyes that young woman who had loved him so much, planting himself at her door to beg her pardon for his rudeness.

― Look, Tomasito, you had me mad with love, but now I’ve tired of it, and you’ll have to beg my pardon for three years, the same as the years I longed after you, and when the last day has passed, if I’m still interested, I may let you give me a kiss.

Of what happened after that, I know nothing.

― I’m afraid I’ll settle for the basic knowledge for defence; the rest, which has brought you so much harm, we’ll leave alone.

― You do well, Milagros; as a good witch I knew you’d take that decision. But before the lesson ends, I’m going to confer a gift on you, since I see your future far from home and I won’t be able to come to your aid at a distance.

Jennifer Esmeralda drew a circle on the floor with a piece of chalk. The candles lit the room. I was on my knees, a white shirt covering my body. She conjured, turning around me; she cut off a lock of my hair and threw it on the embers, poured over me a ewer of whitish water, then began to beat me until my head was bruised, and with the point of a knife she cut me behind the ear, making a mark. I thought I would faint at any moment from sheer pain.

― You may get up now. The hair is the offering so that your guides watch over you. The water purifies you so that evil does not nest. The blows I gave you, life will not give you, and the scar will be your amulet.

After that last session, magic closed its chapter to make way for another great episode with my half-sister. She knew about my homosexuality, and talking about how many maricas and tortilleras (queers and dykes) there were in the neighbourhood, she mentioned Rolando Augusto, the hairdresser, a young lad very well known in the village. He was so stylish that they said even to shit he took up a catwalk pose. The poor thing was so womanish that one day two men armed with knives came to rob his business, but in his flight he jumped the four steps at the entrance down to the ground and sprained both ankles, ending up stretched out on the floor calling for help in tears; and he wept and cried out so much that the robbers, taking pity, lifted him into their car and drove him to the health centre.

― I heard something about that, but I don’t pay much attention to gossip. Incidentally, he limps a little, but he seems to have recovered; I saw him on Saturday at the discotheque with a group of lads that Germán Enrique was in.

― …WHAT…?

― …

― You saw my son in the same discotheque you go to?

― Yes -she looked at me with fire in her eyes-. I had put my foot in it right up to the knee; I had thought she already knew that Germán Enrique is as much a marica as I am. A witch ought to know it, unless she doesn’t want to know.

I felt dreadful, and she kept silent until, days later, he visited us again. He appeared at the door; there were no hugs and no kisses.

― Sit down there.

― What’s the matter, mother?

― Do you like men? -his eyes opened like saucers and he looked at me accusingly. He lowered his head and nodded-. Out of my sight -the lad took the next bus back.

― I don’t know why she gets so angry; I’m a lesbian and she speaks to me. We’re half-sisters; if she does that to her son she’s doing it to me.

She said no more, and the subject was not mentioned again that summer. I know that from that day a couple of years went by without them speaking, but Jennifer Esmeralda fell ill for some weeks and the only child who was with her day and night until she recovered was her youngest, whom she begged to forgive her and loved again with boundless madness.

I know very well that it was he who stayed beside her in the worst moments, until the day the witch crossed to the other side.

The other amusement that kept me busy was my growing fondness for association football and the matches I refereed, more and more of them. At one of them I met Natalia Raquel, a referee I happened upon one Sunday while trophies were being handed out to the children. The first thing that struck me about her were her black eyes, her golden skin, her harmonious face; then the tenderness and the sureness with which she spoke. We started talking on the bench, we ended up having a coffee every now and then, and one afternoon the conversation ended in a kiss. We began going out and feeling love grow.

To our surprise, when the summer was over and the sporting season beginning, the federation called us up to officiate matches in the professional league. So, knowing my papá was well looked after, I set aside the engineering I had wanted and climbed onto a coach beside the woman I loved, rolling from one country to another, rising and rising until I became one of the three women referees in Latin America recognised by FIFA and called upon to officiate top-flight competition. I still keep the trophies I won and the official referee’s shirt.

Those were very lovely years. I travelled and came to know the great continent, I fell head over heels in love and I could see myself on television running up and down with a whistle round my neck. My life had stopped being that of a skivvy looking after old people, children, the poor and a great many brothers. At last I could decide for myself, so much so that even my beloved music began to sound distant.

Within a matter of months my life in the barrio del Carmen seemed too far away. And yet, for all the great happiness that unexpected turn brought me, the years went by and the monotony became tangible again, tied to the fact that the couple was beginning to crack and my life seemed to be losing its meaning. I remembered my half-sister’s words, “a long way round and a great leap”, so I made up my mind to take the riskiest decision and perhaps the most fitting one if I wanted to leave that America that so bored me. I could have chosen China, Russia, Canada, Morocco and let myself be carried off by a change of culture, but I decided on Spain, whose language I shared, and which as a first step towards my inward search seemed a good one.

With no partner now, my job left behind and my possessions sold, I got on a plane bound for Milan. I was thirty-four at the moment I set foot in Europe for the first time. From the sky it looked different, and by God it was going to be!

I was dressed like the richest grand lady in Colombia, as beautiful as any. In my purse my passport and a wad of notes coming to five thousand dollars. When, waiting for my cases to come out, they failed to appear, the flight to Madrid was leaving in six hours, so I had time to make the complaint, with the bad luck that they must have taken me for one of the mafia’s great ladies, and I ended up being pawed from top to bottom by a slobbering policeman who seemed to be enjoying himself thoroughly while he confirmed that there was nothing suspicious about my person.

I spent a good while in the offices until, an hour before the plane left, they let me go. I could wait for the cases to appear or take a flight and leave Italy, giving them up for lost. I took the circumstances in my stride; I had money enough to buy more clothes, there was no problem. I was flying to Spain and the joy of it made me forget what had happened. On reaching the entry border I was the last to get to the window. There was a gentleman with a moustache, from the national guard, very serious, apathetic.

― Passport -I gave it to him-. Ticket -I gave it to him-. Money -I thought about it, but his immovable expression invited me to show him my purse-. Spain is not a country for people like you; do you still wish to enter?

― Yes -I answered, frightened. He took out all the money and put it in his jacket pocket under my astonished gaze-. What are you doing?

― Are you sure you wish to enter? -I could not get over my amazement.

― Of course I am! Give me back my money or I’ll report you.

― Here I am the law. Try it and tomorrow you’ll be back with the sudacas (the spics), after a night in the cells.

― You filthy swine. Give me back one dollar at least so I can make a call and tell my family I arrived safely -he took out a note, felt it, smelled it, slipped it into the passport and handed it back to me.

― Enjoy your stay. I hope to see you again soon.

― Son of a…

I crossed the border and walked with my head high until I reached the exit, where I collapsed in tears, humiliated as I had never been before. There was a long queue waiting for the bus to arrive. There were so many people that, while some got off and others got on, I slipped in through the back door. I kept still, leaning against the window, without looking at a single face, dislodged and furious, so much so that if anyone had spoken to me at that moment I would have spat fire.

At the first stop in the city I got off; I needed room so the rage wouldn’t burst my head. I had lost my money, my luggage, my dignity, and all I could do was scream like a woman possessed along the paths of that little park. When exhaustion got the better of me I sat down and took out the one-dollar note; I cursed my half-sister for giving me protection and luck while I tore the paper into pieces. On the ground, in shreds, looking at it, I felt my inner witch calling for revenge; I picked the note up and kept it, to piece it together some time later and put a spell on the man who had passed his hands over it.

Years later I visited Madrid and went back to that same park, going over that bloody beginning. On the same bench where I had slept that night there was a destitute man clutching a carton of wine, filthy and foul-smelling, asking for alms. I looked at him, he looked at me, we knew each other, I took out the one-dollar note and offered it to him. I don’t think you need to be a witch for life to put everyone in their place.

In short, that night I slept on a bench, out in the open, shivering in a cold proper to the sixteenth of January. The next morning, frightened, hungry and with nowhere to go, I began to walk in whatever direction, and the first bar I came across, I went into. I suppose my bruised face and swollen eyes said enough about me for the lady who ran the bar to come out from behind the counter: “My girl, whatever is the matter? You’re frozen and shivering!”, she said. She sat me at a table and offered me a coffee, and once I had warmed up I told her what had happened to me in Milan and Madrid. I thanked her for the coffee and asked her for something to eat in exchange for work, washing up, cleaning, fetching or carrying, it made no difference. Without a thought she agreed, and she would have done more had she been able, but she made me the following confession.

― Look, child, I’d take you home so you could sleep under a roof, but my husband wouldn’t allow it; in fact, when he comes in here, you won’t be here. He’s very strict and I don’t want trouble with him. But he has set hours for coming into the bar. He works as a caretaker at a school, so you’ll help me while he’s at work. At night he comes to lock up and we go home together. The bar has a back door, the larder is there, there’s plenty of cardboard, and this afternoon I’ll go and fetch some blankets. Sleeping there on the quiet is the only thing I can offer you until you find something better.

I hugged her, tremendously grateful, and for two weeks I worked on the sly in the bar in exchange for food and a roof. I washed at a sink and wore clothes doña Gloria lent me while mine were being washed. Luck was with me this time and I did not once run into her husband.

On the second day I called my papá and left him the bar’s telephone number, and fourteen days later my brother Elgar Nicolás called to hear how I was. I said nothing of my situation; on the contrary, I told them both I was delighted, though I came close to asking my brother for money and telling him the truth. But the conversation took an unexpected turn just then. The chatter led him to mention one of the girlfriends he had had in Spain when he was there years back, in a place called Orihuela. So he hung up the telephone and a while later rang again to say he had managed to speak to her and that I should go to that town, where she would meet me and help me find work. I thanked him, delighted; I knew nobody in Madrid, so finding myself beside a compatriot who was a friend of my brother’s gave me breathing space. I told señora Gloria, gave her a thousand kisses for those days of generosity, and she made me a present of the money to pay for the coach ticket. Like a good witch, I wished her all the happiness in the world. We kept ringing each other afterwards to hear how we were; her husband died soon after, so she lived many happy and free years. But I promise I had nothing to do with it.

The coach brought me to the town in question, and there, at the bus station, I waited for my brother’s friend to appear. Five in the afternoon turned into eleven at night with nobody in sight. I could do nothing but curse my luck. Seeing I would be sleeping in the street again, I went into the building; there was still one window with the light on and a girl inside sorting papers. I went over to her and with a certain humour I told her my adventures. She was wary.

― I’m not deceiving you. See my cases? Neither do I -and turning out my pockets-, see any money? Not a penny -she was thinking about it.

― Come in, but you can’t stay here, I’m waiting for the last coach so I can lock up. There’s a sandwich in the fridge, it’s yours; all the water you want in the dispenser. If you have anyone to call, use it, but be brief, please. She went on with her work while I devoured the ham sandwich. I dialled my brother’s number over and over, but it was engaged. What I wanted was to go home. Half past eleven was about to strike.

― Milagros, I’m very sorry to tell you, but the coach has just come in; once the driver clocks off, we close and we go. I have fifteen hundred pesetas on me; sleep in a hostel tonight and come back tomorrow, and with luck I’ll slip you onto a bus back to Madrid, and there you can wait for your flight home. I don’t know what else to tell you.

― Don’t you worry, you’ve done enough already, I am most grateful to you. There are only seven days until my flight, it’s no great matter.

The back door opened and a bald man, dark, on the plump side, came in with a smile, clocked off and left the keys, wished us good night and with an odd look noticed me.

― Hey, how are you! What brings you here? What a delight, everything all right -I looked at him, unable to get over my astonishment.

― I don’t think we know each other.

― What do you mean you don’t know me? And what’s the matter, that I see you looking so glum?

― Oh, what do you want me to say! After what I’ve been through these past days I don’t know myself in the mirror, so if we do know each other, it’s a blessing.

― Well of course we do! Let’s see, picture me with a good head of black hair, put a bushy moustache on me and take this great belly away -I looked at him, no memory coming-. I’m Álvaro.

― …Álvaro…? From where?

― I drove that van that carried you all up and down through the season, and on the way to Maracaibo you wound down the window and stuck your backside out in the open air -the memory came back all at once; I got up with a loud shout of joy and threw my arms around him.

― What a delight, Álvaro, to see you here!

― Off we go, then, and over dinner, which is on me, you’ll tell me what brought you here.

I thanked the girl for her kindness and we left the station behind. Álvaro took me to his house and, while he made dinner, with a kindness that won me over, he let me shower, tidy myself, relax, and lent me some clothes. I sat down at the table and he was waiting for me with everything served. Over dinner he listened very closely to my account and, once he had recovered from his disbelief, he said.

― Milagros, that was bad luck, but it’s over. Whenever you like, you can stay with me; you’ll sleep on that sofa bed and I’ll help you find work, I know a lot of people around here. Don’t be uneasy in the house, I spend many hours out and some nights I sleep in hotels in other towns, so make yourself at home and don’t hesitate to ask me for anything.

Living with him, setting the strangeness aside, turned out pleasant. A couple of days later, settled in the town now, I went to the referees’ college meaning to find a little work in something I knew how to do. When I got there I let them know my hoja de vida (my CV) and they were open-mouthed, and since they didn’t believe it I invited them to send a fax to the International Federation of Football; the record came through confirming what I knew. They were willing to take me on, but they could only urge me to come back when I was legal in Spain, so I went back thinking about Colombia again. It is very hard to know yourself a capable person, sound in your morals, and to feel yourself a fugitive from the law for being illegal; it is a feeling that frightens, when all you want is to work, do no harm and be happy. You suffer a great deal once the six months of a tourist permit are past and you come across an officer of the authorities. An inhuman situation, made worse when you have to deal with banks, administrations or health services. Water under the bridge.

In the same street as the house, Álvaro was coming the other way with a smile.

― Milagros! Great news, I have a job for you. It’s nothing much, they’ll pay you little, no contract, but at least you’ll have money for a small rent and enough to eat and dress. This town is very quiet, so if all goes well you’ll be able to get your papers in time. It will be hard, but if it’s what you want it will be worth it.

Within a week and thanks to his boundless generosity, I found an old little flat in a hamlet ten minutes from the town where the rent was cheaper, and I got a job as an odd-job girl in a roadside bar and hostel an hour and five minutes’ walk from my home. I decided to swap the running of refereeing for the long walks to work. Ten or eleven hours I spent shut in there; I stopped for one hour and they fed me, and if anything was left over, supper too. I worked without a break and with great effort, for it was always full of travelling salesmen. I earned about two thousand pesetas, which was little compared with the lowest paid, the waiters, who were on about six thousand a day. The truth is I thought long and hard about whether I wanted to stay in Spain. The beginning had been arduous and promised to go on being so; I was far from home, alone, with no football, no music, nothing familiar, working and feeling myself a beggar. But in Colombia I already knew how my life would go on: my father, the orchestra, perhaps the engineering, looking after my mother’s legacy. I wanted to give myself the chance to see what happened if I carried on with what I had started, and I decided to stay.

With the first wage I earned, I paid Álvaro back the money he had lent me and took him out to dinner along with the girl from the ticket window. That dinner for three was good fun and, although it wasn’t among friends, it was among people who spilled over with humanity. I went on like that for two years and, fortunately, I managed to make my situation legal. By then I was tired of being the restaurant’s skivvy and thinking about refereeing matches in the second division, but that night, on my way home, crossing the square, an old man killing time on a bench caught my attention.

― Girl…

― Me?

― Yes, you, the dark-skinned one, come over, please.

― Tell me, how can I help you?

― Will you sit with me five minutes and talk? -I sat down beside him and listened-. How’s it going, being the foreign girl in the village?

― Well, I’ve felt no rejection. Perhaps at first they all looked at me more, but nothing that has made me feel uncomfortable. Getting used to my new life was hard enough that I didn’t stop to think about whether they liked my being here or not.

― In this village there have always been summer foreigners living in their little houses, so your arrival, though permanent and different, hasn’t given us much to talk about. Are you thinking of staying longer?

― I don’t know, it may be time to change tack.

― I suppose it’s time to leave the restaurant and the fugitive’s life.

― When I was illegal, anything would do; now that I can say I’m Spanish, I think I deserve at least decent treatment.

― Dignity shouldn’t have borders or a passport. On the other hand, if you want to stay, I’ll offer you an alternative. Years ago I opened a bar, on that corner; my wife died and my children lost interest, and since I retired there it is, closed, full of dust, useless. It’s all mine, there are no debts, it was a good bar and there’s nothing I’d like more than to see it open again, working, hearing the men’s voices while the dominoes clack on the table… Are we doing well so far?

― Go on.

― I give you the keys to the bar, you run it from seven in the morning until ten at night, you’re your own boss and I make the first order of stock. If it works again, you carry on running it; if not, you lose your time and your effort, and I lose my money.

― It sounds like a good deal, but what do you get out of it?

― To be “young” again.

― And if it all works, I can live off the bar, and in the future I’d want it to be mine.

― In that case you only have to buy it. The will is made: two million pesetas, one for each child. Even if I died tomorrow, which I don’t expect to, there would be no complications -he offered me his hand; I didn’t wait a moment to think it over and shook it firmly.

― Done.

A few days later I started by cleaning the bar, and when it was gleaming and full of stock, we reopened. For the first few days nobody came in. He sat at a table and I stood behind the counter; now and then somebody came in to buy tobacco and, with luck, had a beer. The first two months were maddening, but little by little we got the neighbours into the habit and turned them into a clientele that at least saw us to the end of the month without trouble. Then summer came and the village filled with English, Germans, Norwegians; from opening to closing the bar was packed with two-metre blonds drinking beer and eating my attempts at Spanish food. We discovered that with what was saved over the summer it didn’t matter if the winter was thin.

Some years later I had learned to cook, and seeing that the bar was working I made up my mind to buy it. I paid what we had agreed and it became mine. Severiano sold it to me delighted, and seeing his bar full of life he kept me company every day, sitting in his corner, until he died. I felt comfortable, the owner of my own business; I had my car and I bought myself a little house. It seemed that the life I had been looking for when I left Colombia behind was to be found in this little village.

Here I had no time to stop. I saw expensive champagne, seafood and caviar, drugs, whores and the kind of betting that runs to a great deal of money, just like the sort that ruined my grandfather. Fights between women pulling each other’s hair, drunks and muggers, almost everything. But what I saw and read most was the papers every morning.

I must have been about thirty-seven when my brother rang to tell me that my papá, don Sergio Alejandro, had died at a hundred and four years of age, in good spirits and playing the trumpet. I felt sad and far from my own, and looking at the sky I said goodbye to my papá, asking his forgiveness for not being there at his farewell. I had been left an orphan, and the feeling of loneliness made itself present and knocked me flat; I hadn’t stopped to think that I was alone. All at once I realised I needed to love, and as though by chance, while I was leafing through the paper, my eyes came to rest on a message that read “young woman interested in meeting another woman for friendship or possible relationship”. I could do nothing but burst out laughing; in Colombia, meeting people of your own tastes was easier than writing telegrams in the newspaper. The upshot was that I answered the message, and the next day my own words appeared: “stop looking, because you’ve already found me”.

No sooner said than done: that Friday we met, we talked, we had dinner and the thing is we liked each other. A few months later we went further, we liked it even more, and so within the year she came to live with me. We loved each other and enjoyed each other for half a lifetime, until she died a year ago. I haven’t long left; I know when my turn is, I feel it close now, and the truth is I’m waiting gladly to be with my papás again, my wife, my brothers and sisters. Jennifer Esmeralda was right when she said I would find my fortune on the other side of the sea; though the beginning was disheartening, the rest came easily, and I can say I have been greatly happy.

Rosalinda Camila works here now. She’s a young girl, Guatemalan and now Spanish, an immigrant. I made her the same deal Severiano made me all those years ago, so that when I die she’ll be able to carry on with this lifeline for lost people.
